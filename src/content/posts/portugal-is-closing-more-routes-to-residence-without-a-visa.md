Portugal has approved further changes to its immigration law that remove two more possibilities for applying for residence after entering the country without a residence visa.

The changes affect people who hoped to obtain residence by enrolling in a professional training course after arriving in Portugal. They also restrict the rule that allowed foreign parents to apply for residence because they had a minor child legally residing in the country. Under the approved wording, this route will be limited to parents of Portuguese children.

These changes still need to complete the legislative process before they become fully effective. Even so, the message for anyone planning to move to Portugal is already clear: entering as a tourist and hoping to deal with immigration matters later is becoming a very poor strategy.

## Legal entry as a tourist does not give you the right to live in Portugal

I regularly speak with people who were told that they could travel to Portugal without a residence visa, settle here and find a way to apply for residence afterwards.

That advice often ignores the difference between entering Portugal legally and having a legal basis to remain here.

A person may be entitled to enter Portugal without a visa for a short visit. This does not automatically allow that person to establish residence, work or submit a residence permit application from within the country.

The problem usually becomes apparent only after the move has already taken place. By then, the person may have rented a home, brought their family, enrolled a child in school or committed a considerable amount of money to the relocation.

Discovering at that stage that there is no valid route to residence can mean having to leave Portugal and begin the correct visa process from abroad.

## Portugal is removing the routes people used to rely on after arrival

The possibility of entering Portugal first and regularizing one’s status later has already been significantly reduced.

The expression of interest procedure, which allowed many people to apply for residence based on employment or self-employment after entering Portugal, was abolished in 2024.

Residence permits for CPLP nationals now generally require a prior residence visa. The government has also moved to end the use of professional training courses as a way to obtain residence after arrival and to restrict residence applications made by parents of foreign children residing in Portugal.

There are still specific situations in which Portuguese law allows a residence permit without a prior residence visa. Those are exceptions created for particular circumstances. They should not be treated as a general immigration plan or as an alternative available to anyone who enters Portugal as a tourist.

## A residence visa is already the first part of the residence process

Many applicants see the residence visa as nothing more than an entry document. In practice, it serves a much more important purpose.

A Portuguese residence visa is issued because the applicant has already presented a legal reason for moving to Portugal. Depending on the case, that reason may be employment, remote work, passive income, retirement, business activity, study or family reunification.

The visa allows the person to enter Portugal as someone intending to become a resident, rather than as a visitor hoping to find a solution after arrival.

When a residence visa is issued, the Portuguese Ministry of Foreign Affairs normally assigns an appointment for the residence permit application before AIMA. The appointment details are usually connected to the visa itself. When no appointment is available or the link does not work, the visa holder has a specific AIMA channel through which the appointment can be requested.

This is why describing the transition from a residence visa to a residence permit as an additional service or a special advantage can be misleading. The transition is already part of the structure of a Portuguese residence visa.

The visa does not guarantee that the residence permit will be granted. The applicant must still satisfy the applicable requirements before AIMA. It does, however, provide the correct legal route for entering Portugal and beginning that process.

## Applying for the correct visa gives you a safer start

A residence visa application takes preparation. Depending on the category, the applicant may need to prove income, savings, accommodation, employment, professional activity, enrollment in an educational institution or family ties.

For that reason, some people are tempted to travel first and deal with the paperwork later. What appears easier at the beginning can quickly become far more expensive and complicated.

The appropriate route may be a D7 visa for someone supported by passive income, a D8 visa for a remote professional, a D2 visa for an entrepreneur, a work visa, a study visa or a family reunification visa. The correct choice depends on the applicant’s income, professional situation, nationality, family circumstances and plans in Portugal.

This should be assessed before booking a one-way flight, signing a long-term lease or making financial commitments that depend on being able to remain in the country.

## Portugal still offers several legal routes for new residents

These restrictions do not mean that Portugal is closed to foreign residents. There are still suitable immigration routes for professionals, entrepreneurs, remote workers, retirees, students and families.

What has changed is the amount of room available for improvisation.

Anyone seriously planning to move to Portugal should begin by identifying the appropriate visa, confirming the requirements and preparing the application before traveling. Waiting until after arrival may leave the person with fewer options, greater costs and no legal basis to remain.
